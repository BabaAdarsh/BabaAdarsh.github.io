<b>Mind in Motion</b>, post six. We keep returning to one idea: the brain predicts the consequences of its own actions. This post follows that idea to a surprising place, the feeling that you are the one moving at all.
</div>

Here is a small experiment you can run right now. Try to tickle yourself. Go on, dig into your own ribs the way a friend would. Nothing. A faint pressure, maybe, but not the helpless, squirming tickle you would get if someone else did exactly the same thing. Your own fingers, the same motion, and yet the sensation is gone.

That failure is not trivial. It is a window onto one of the deepest tricks the brain performs, the one that lets you know, without ever thinking about it, that you are the author of your own movements.

## The sense you never notice until it slips

Most of the time you have an effortless feeling of agency: the sense that your actions are yours, that the hand reaching for the cup is being moved by you and not by some outside force. It is so automatic that it seems too obvious to need explaining. But it does need explaining, because the brain has no direct line to the truth of who caused what. It has to work it out, and the way it works it out is prediction.

We have already met the machinery. When you send a motor command, the brain also sends a copy of that command, an efference copy, to a forward model that predicts the sensory consequences (Sperry, 1950; von Holst and Mittelstaedt, 1950). If the sensations that arrive match the prediction, the brain concludes: this was me. And it does something telling with that conclusion. It turns the volume down.

Blakemore, Wolpert and Frith showed this directly. A tactile stimulus is felt as less intense and less ticklish when you produce it yourself than when a machine delivers exactly the same stimulus, and brain imaging traced the difference to the cerebellum predicting, and cancelling, the sensory consequences of the movement (Blakemore, Wolpert and Frith, 1998). The tickle is missing because your brain saw it coming.

This same cancellation quietly runs your whole life. When your eyes flick across this page, the world does not appear to lurch, even though the image on your retina is swinging wildly, because the brain predicts the visual consequences of its own eye movements and discounts them. You do not startle at the sound of your own footsteps. Prediction is constantly subtracting the parts of your experience that you caused, leaving the parts that the world caused, which are the parts worth paying attention to.

## When the boundary breaks

Now for the part that turns a party trick into something profound. If your sense of agency is built by predicting and cancelling your own actions, what happens when that prediction system fails?

The unsettling answer is that your own movements can start to feel as though someone else is making them. In schizophrenia, some people experience exactly this: the conviction that their limbs are being moved by an external force, that their actions are not their own. A leading account, developed by Chris Frith and colleagues, is that these passivity experiences reflect a breakdown in predicting the consequences of self-generated action (Frith, Blakemore and Wolpert, 2000). Consistent with that idea, people with these symptoms show reduced sensory attenuation: unlike the rest of us, they can, to a degree, tickle themselves (Blakemore et al., 2000). The comparator that should whisper this was me has gone quiet, and without it, the self can feel like a stranger.

<div class="deeper">
<span class="deeper-label">Going deeper</span>
<p>It is worth separating two things the literature often braids together: the sense of agency (that I caused an action) and the sense of body ownership (that this limb is mine). Both draw on multisensory prediction and integration, and both can be manipulated, as in the rubber-hand illusion for ownership. Sensory attenuation is one measurable signature of the prediction that supports agency, though its interpretation is debated, and not every account ties it neatly to a forward model. What is robust is the core logic: distinguishing self from world is an inference, and inferences can fail.</p>
</div>

## Why a missing tickle is worth taking seriously

The reason I find this corner of neuroscience so compelling is that it collapses a false divide. We tend to think of motor control as plumbing, the business of getting muscles to move, and of selfhood as something loftier and separate. But the feeling that you are the one acting turns out to be made of the very same predictive machinery that steers your reaches and stabilizes your gaze. The self, at least this thin but essential slice of it, is a side effect of a control system predicting itself.

So the next time you fail to tickle yourself, notice what that failure means. Your brain just proved, silently, that it knew what you were about to do, because it was the one doing it. That quiet certainty is not something you have. It is something your nervous system builds, prediction by prediction, and it is more fragile, and more wondrous, than it feels.
